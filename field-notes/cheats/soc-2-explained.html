SOC 2 Explained: What It Is and What It Changes

When my employer first said we were pursuing SOC 2, I had no idea what it was. I assumed it was SOX, which I already knew, and I was wrong. Once I learned what was really involved, I panicked.

At first my productivity tanked: new techniques, new skills, new policies. We retired our SSH suite and moved to AWS Systems Manager (SSM), and with the help of custom AI tools I rebuilt my workflow into something efficient. I'm still not the greatest fan of SOC 2, but I think the practices are a net positive, and I think my panic was warranted.

SOC 2 is a voluntary audit report on how a company protects its customers' data. It is not a law and not a certificate, but the work behind it is real, and it changes how everyone in the company does their job. In my view, no one can keep doing business as usual once a company commits to it.

This article is for employees whose company has just announced SOC 2, and it covers three things worth knowing before the work starts.

  2. Who uses SOC 2, and whether it works
  3. What SOC 2 changes for you

1What SOC 2 Is, and What It Is Not

It is a report, not a certificate

The AICPA describes System and Organization Controls (SOC) as "a suite of service offerings CPAs may provide in connection with system-level controls of a service organization." A CPA firm examines the controls a company says it has and issues an opinion. One audit firm puts it bluntly: a SOC 2 is technically an attestation report, and "it's not a certification." A Type 1 report tests the design of the controls at one point in time; a Type 2 report tests how they operated over a period, usually 12 months.

It is a market expectation, not a law

Secureframe, a compliance vendor, states that "SOC 2 reports are not legally required." The need comes from customers: Vanta's 2025 Trust Maturity Report says 83% of enterprise buyers require a SOC 2 report before signing with a new vendor, rising to 91% at companies with more than 5,000 employees. Vanta sells compliance software, and the page does not give the sample size, so read the number as a signal of how buyers behave.

This is also where I made my mistake. SOX is a law: the Sarbanes-Oxley Act was enacted in 2002 after the Enron and WorldCom scandals, and its Section 404 requires public company management to assess and report on its internal control over financial reporting, with an independent auditor attesting to that assessment. SOC 2 is about protecting customer data, and nothing requires a company to do it.

It is a framework, not a checklist

SOC 2 does not hand a company a list of steps. The same audit firm says there is no checklist, because the criteria "may be met in a variety of ways," so each company chooses the controls it will be tested on. That is why two companies with SOC 2 reports can run very differently.

It also does not guarantee security. In the audit firm's words, "SOC 2 compliance does not ensure data security." A report shows that the basics were in place and operating; it does not promise that nothing will go wrong.

2Who Uses SOC 2, and Whether It Works

Who uses it, and who does not

SOC 2 is aimed at service organizations that handle customer data, and it is especially expected of SaaS providers and cloud-based businesses. Secureframe's 2026 benchmark report found that 61% of respondents needed compliance to win or renew a contract. That survey covers several frameworks, not SOC 2 alone.

Other organizations use a different report or none. Secureframe points providers whose services affect a customer's financial reporting, such as payroll, to a SOC 1 report; audiences who do not need system detail to a public SOC 3 report; and manufacturers and distributors to a SOC for Supply Chain report. A company with no customer data to protect, or no business customers asking, has little reason to run an audit.

How trendy it is

The strongest demand figures come from companies that sell SOC 2 software, which is worth knowing before you repeat them. I found no independent count of how many companies hold a SOC 2 report, and one often-repeated adoption figure had no traceable source, so I left it out. The demand is real, but the numbers are better read as a sales signal than a measurement.

How practical it is

The practical case is the cost of getting security wrong. IBM's 2025 study of 600 organizations, conducted by the Ponemon Institute, put the global average cost of a breach at $4.44 million and the U.S. average at a record $10.22 million. Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report, covering over 22,000 incidents and 12,195 confirmed breaches, found that third-party involvement doubled to 30%, with credential abuse (22%) and exploited vulnerabilities (20%) the leading ways in.

Those are the areas SOC 2 controls cover: who has access, how vulnerabilities are handled, and how vendors are vetted. I found no study showing that holding a SOC 2 report lowers a company's breach rate, so I treat it as a reasonable discipline, not a proven shield. In my experience the discipline is worth having.

3What SOC 2 Changes for You

Your workflow changes

Expect new tools and a slower start. Retiring our SSH suite for SSM meant relearning routine tasks, and my output dropped before it recovered. A 2026 study of 75 completed examinations, reported by Bright Defense, found that first-time audits took a median of 9 weeks of fieldwork against 7 for renewals, with 2.1 rounds of evidence clarification against 1.3. The first pass is the hardest for the auditors and for the people who produce the evidence.

AI tools helped me through it, and I doubt I would have managed as well without custom ones. They carry their own risk, though: IBM found that 97% of the organizations that reported a breach of an AI model or application had no AI access controls in place. Ask which AI tools are approved before you put work data into one.

Your shortcuts end

A Type 2 report tests controls over a period, usually 12 months, not on one good day. In my view, that means a shortcut that used to be harmless, such as a shared login or a manual fix in production, becomes something an auditor can find. People who assume they can work as before are the ones who run into trouble.

I also think management sometimes softens the specifics to keep the peace and avoid panic. I understand the instinct, but I would rather have been told early, because my panic led me to learn the details. Ask for the specifics of what changes in your role.

Your devices are in scope

Any device that reaches company systems can fall inside the audit, including a personal phone used only for sign-in. I now keep my work equipment completely separate from my personal devices. The reasons and the risks are in the BYOD field note.

Your Task

SOC 2 is not a law, a certificate, or a guarantee; it is a CPA's report on whether your company does what it says it does to protect customer data. It is also a real change in how you work, and the best response is to take it seriously and get specific. I did, and I am better for it.

Before your company's audit period starts, put these five items on your list:

  1. Ask which systems, accounts, and devices in your role are in scope.
  2. Ask which of your current habits the new policies replace.
  3. Ask when the audit period starts and what evidence you are expected to leave behind.
  4. Ask which AI tools are approved before you use them on work data.
  5. Read the policies once in full, and keep a copy.

If an answer is vague, ask again, and ask in writing. Your company is about to be tested on what its people do, and you are one of those people.
