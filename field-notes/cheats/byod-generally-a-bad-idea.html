BYOD: Generally, a Bad Idea

For seven years I did my job from a personal MacBook, iPhone, and iPad. Then our tech team was tasked with making our systems SOC 2 compliant, and I learned what that would ask of those devices. I requested a work-issued MacBook, and after some resistance it was approved.

Moving off my iPhone was the hard part. Slack and email on the go were gone, and so were the authenticator apps I used for two-factor sign-in (Authy and Google Authenticator); a YubiKey workflow replaced them. I gave up real conveniences, and I would make the same call again, because that boundary should not have been crossed in the first place.

Bring your own device (BYOD) means doing your job on a phone or laptop you own and pay for. It offers you convenience, but it hands your employer management authority over your device: depending on how it is configured, that can range from managing work apps to erasing the whole phone. In my view, BYOD is generally a bad idea, because your employer gets the savings while you take on the cost and the risk, and because mixing personal and work use blurs a boundary that is hard to restore.

This article is for employees who have been asked to enroll a personal device. It covers three things that decide whether the trade is fair.

  1. What “in scope” means for your devices
  2. What MDM can see and do on them
  3. What you give up when you enroll

1What “In Scope” Means for Your Devices

What “in scope” means

The AICPA describes System and Organization Controls (SOC) as “a suite of service offerings CPAs may provide in connection with system-level controls of a service organization.” The company writes a description of its system, and the auditor tests whether the controls in it are designed well (Type 1) or operated effectively over a period of time (Type 2), according to Venn's overview of a SOC 2 report. The system boundary decides what that description, and so the testing, covers.

“In scope” means inside that boundary. One exam-prep guide describes the boundary as a perimeter that encompasses all system components used to provide the services: infrastructure, software, people, procedures, and data. A scoping guide says to start by asking which accounts can materially affect the in-scope system, so scope follows access paths, not who owns the hardware.

For devices, one practitioner guide puts it plainly: any laptop, phone, or tablet used to access service data falls under scope. In my reading, a personal device is not under the company's direct control, so the company has to bring it under control with policy and software before an auditor will accept it. That is where management software comes from.

A phone used only for two-factor sign-in can be in scope. One vendor's BYOD policy template limits a personal phone to hosting an approved authenticator, with controls such as a screen lock and a supported operating system, and no device trust granted. Another vendor guide says personal phones used for company email or cloud apps must enroll in MDM and allow remote wipe. In scope does not mean fully managed: your employer's policy decides how far the controls reach, so a sign-in phone may carry little exposure or the same exposure as a computer. I use a YubiKey for sign-in so no authenticator app on a personal phone is needed.

What SOC 2 does not prescribe

One practitioner guide states that SOC 2 has no universal rule that every device must be company-owned. The company decides how to protect its data, and the auditor tests what the company says it does. The criteria most often tied to devices are the logical and physical access group, CC6.1 through CC6.8 (Barr Advisory).

A company that allows personal devices still has to show it controls access to its data on them. Vendors that sell this software say auditors look for evidence such as enrollment, baseline settings, and revocation, and that removing company data when someone leaves is hard to prove.

Many employers choose not to allow personal devices at all. In Verizon's 2025 Mobile Security Index, a survey of 762 professionals, 55% of organizations said they provide company-owned devices, 19% use a mix, and 25% allow personal devices. The same report says 70% of mobile devices hit by an attack were personal, not corporate-issued; that does not show personal devices are riskier one for one, because the report does not say how many devices of each kind were in use.

What the explanation leaves out

The sources I reviewed describe audit evidence as enrollment records and removal of company data. I found nothing that makes an employee's understanding of the trade part of that evidence. In my view, that is why the explanation often shrinks to a policy you click to accept.

2What MDM Can See and Do on Your Devices

What MDM is

Mobile device management (MDM) is software that puts a management profile on your device and takes instructions from your employer's server. The company uses it to require a passcode and encryption, install work apps, and remove them again. On a personally owned Apple device, the preferred mode is User Enrollment, where Apple says IT can manage only an organization's accounts, settings, and information, never a personal account, and can apply only a limited set of restrictions.

What a computer adds

A computer holds far more of your life than a phone: your files, saved passwords, browser sessions, and the data from every other account you use. It also runs software with deeper access to all of that. So the same policy that is modest on a phone can reach further on a computer, and a mistake costs more.

Compliance agents show the range. Vanta's agent queries installed applications, browser extensions, local user accounts, disk encryption, screen lock, and the operating system version, and says it does not send passwords, SSH keys, messages, or browsing history. Drata's agent reads the installed app and browser extension lists and the serial number, and says it does not modify files. Neither page says whether the agent is meant for personal computers.

Full management can go further. Apple says Device Enrollment also supervises a Mac running macOS 11 or later, and Microsoft lists file paths, registry and event log entries, and running processes among the details it can query on a corporate-owned Windows computer. Those entries describe company-owned devices, so ask which category your computer would fall into and what you would have to install.

App lists are a line many people draw. A 2012 Harris survey of 2,243 enterprise workers, commissioned by MDM vendor Fiberlink, found that 76% would not give their employer access to the apps installed on a personal device. The survey is old and vendor-sponsored, so treat the number as a signal, not a measurement.

What the enrollment type decides

Microsoft's Intune documentation lists what the organization can never see: calling and web browsing history, email and text messages, contacts, calendar, passwords, pictures, and the content of your documents. It can always see the device owner, name, serial number, model, manufacturer, operating system version, and IMEI. On a personal device it can also see the last four digits of your phone number and your managed app inventory, but not your location.

Microsoft adds that some configurations let organizations see more than the managed apps on a personal device. Apple's full Device Enrollment is built for organization-owned devices and includes the ability to erase the device.

Note: "We use MDM" tells you very little. The limits above belong to the enrollment type your employer picks, and the employer picks it, not you.

3What You Give Up When You Enroll

Control of your own device

You accept rules on something you own: a passcode policy, required updates, and restrictions on some settings. A 2015 Harris poll of more than 3,500 mobile workers, commissioned by MDM vendor MobileIron, found that only 61% believed their employer could keep their personal information private, and 30% said they would quit if their employer could view it. Your employer may well respect the limits it describes; the point is that those limits come from its configuration, which it can change, and from its word.

Exposure to legal and data-loss risk

Most courts treat work data on personal devices as subject to the same preservation duties as other electronic data. If your employer faces a lawsuit, it may have to preserve and collect work-related data from your device, and you may be asked to cooperate. The reverse risk is a remote action: on a device enrolled more broadly than you expected, the management server may be able to erase everything, and on a computer that is more of your files, photos, and work than a phone holds.

Cost you may not recover

You pay for the device, the plan, and the repairs, and a computer costs more to replace than a phone. In California, the Court of Appeal held in Cochran v. Schwan's Home Service (2014) that an employer must reimburse a reasonable percentage of the phone bill when employees are required to use a personal phone for work, in a case covering about 1,500 service managers. Other states differ, and the ruling concerns required use of a phone, so a voluntary BYOD program, or a personal computer, may fall outside it.

Your Task

BYOD is a trade, and your employer sets the terms. Whether the device is a phone used only for sign-in or a computer used all day, being in scope means your employer's policy reaches it, and you decide how far only by asking. Decide on purpose, before you tap Accept.

Before you enroll, put these six questions to your IT team and keep the answers in writing:

  1. Which of my devices are in scope for SOC 2, including a phone I would use only for sign-in?
  2. Which enrollment type or software agent do you use, and what can it see on my device?
  3. What can you do remotely: remove only work data, or erase the whole device?
  4. What happens to my device in a legal hold or an investigation?
  5. What will this cost me, and do you offer a stipend or reimbursement?
  6. What happens to my device when I leave, and which company-issued device is the alternative?

If every answer is clear, written, and acceptable to you, BYOD can be a fair choice. If any answer is vague, ask for a company device, a hardware key for sign-in, or a separate low-cost phone for work, and keep your personal devices yours. Asking for a company device is a reasonable request, not a difficult one.
